**Case theme**

I exercised workplace rights by raising formal employment-related complaints in November 2025 and by making a written flexible work request on 12 January 2026 because of changed childcare responsibilities. After that, my work was closely scrutinised, the 23 January meeting shifted from a flexible work discussion into a termination-risk performance meeting, and the later review conditions confined me to ordinary business hours only. I say that combination created general protections risk for the employer.

**What I need to get across**

My case is not that employers can never manage performance. My case is that, on these facts, the timing, the change in treatment after protected conduct, the use of informal Jira estimates as if they were formal benchmarks, and the removal of flexibility I relied on for childcare created legal risk. A flexible work request sits within the workplace-right framework, and workplace rights also include complaints or enquiries about employment. ([Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/employment-conditions/protections-at-work "Protections at work - Fair Work Ombudsman"))

**My 3 strongest factual points**

1. On 12 January 2026 I made a written flexible work request tied to childcare, asking for arrangements to be confirmed before 23 January.
    
2. Before any proper decision on that request, management asked me for detailed work-output information, then further explanations, and then said the 23 January meeting would also more broadly discuss my performance. When I asked what I needed to prepare, I was told it was a continuation of earlier discussions and there was nothing to prepare.
    
3. My notes record that the 23 January meeting was originally scheduled to discuss flexible working arrangements but instead escalated into a performance discussion with termination risk, followed by the 27 January warning letter and the 2 February review conditions requiring performance to be assessed only within ordinary business hours.
    

**Forced resignation point**

I accept I resigned, but my position is that it was not a genuinely voluntary resignation. The Commission says dismissal includes where a person was forced to resign because of the employer’s conduct or course of conduct, although the line is narrow and the employee must prove it. ([Fish and Wildlife Commission](https://www.fwc.gov.au/what-dismissal-0 "What is dismissal? | Fair Work Commission"))

My best version of that point is:

- the review conditions effectively removed flexibility I had been relying on to manage childcare
    
- the pressure and surrounding conduct were seriously affecting my health
    
- continuing in the role was not realistically possible.
    

The medical point supports this, but should not replace the conduct point.

**Medical point**

Use this as support, not the whole case:

“By that stage I was experiencing serious work-related anxiety symptoms, including panic attacks, chest pains, and repeated time off. My doctor and psychologist can corroborate that.”

Do not use “nervous breakdown” unless that exact wording appears in your medical evidence.

**Likely employer response**

They will likely say:

- this was ordinary performance management
    
- performance concerns started in September 2025
    
- the review period was supportive and temporary
    
- your contract already set ordinary hours
    
- you resigned voluntarily.
    

Your answer:

“I understand that is their position. My point is that the timing, the escalation immediately around the flexible work request, the lack of fair notice for the 23 January meeting, the reliance on informal estimates, and the later restriction to ordinary hours created real risk under the general protections provisions.”

**What conciliation is for**

The Commission’s role is to help resolve the dispute, not decide who is right. If no agreement is reached, the Commission can issue a certificate and you then have 14 days to go to court or seek arbitration by consent. ([Fish and Wildlife Commission](https://www.fwc.gov.au/workplace-disputes/general-protections-and-harmful-adverse-action/general-protections-applications "General protections applications involving dismissal | Fair Work Commission"))

**What to have in front of me**

The Commission says to gather relevant material, including the contract, recent payslip, warning or dismissal letters, and application and response forms. For you, the essential bundle is:

- employment contract
    
- flexible work request
    
- 19 to 22 January email chain
    
- 23 January meeting notes
    
- 27 January outcome letter
    
- 2 February review-period email
    
- key November complaint documents
    
- resignation email. ([Fish and Wildlife Commission](https://www.fwc.gov.au/job-loss-or-dismissal/unfair-dismissal/conciliation-unfair-dismissal/tips-prepare-conciliation "Tips to prepare for conciliation | Fair Work Commission"))
    

**Settlement posture**

Know before the call:

- your ideal figure
    
- your realistic figure
    
- your minimum acceptable figure
    
- your key non-money terms.
    

Useful non-money terms may include statement of service, agreed separation wording, confidentiality, non-disparagement, tax wording, super and leave confirmation, and no adverse commentary about performance.

## Speaking script

### 1. Opening summary, about 60 to 90 seconds

“Thanks. My position is that I exercised workplace rights by first raising formal employment-related concerns in November 2025, including concerns about management conduct, burnout and working arrangements, and then by making a written flexible work request on 12 January 2026 because of changed childcare responsibilities. Soon after that request, management began scrutinising my work output in detail, and the meeting on 23 January, which was supposed to address flexible work, shifted into a performance meeting where I was told my employment was at risk. That was then followed by a formal warning and a review process under which my performance would only be assessed based on work completed within ordinary business hours. I say that sequence creates real risk for the employer in a general protections claim.”

### 2. If asked why you say the timing matters

“The timing matters because the flexible work request was made on 12 January due to childcare responsibilities. Before that request was properly addressed, I was asked to provide detailed ticket-by-ticket time information, then further explanations, and then I was told the next meeting would also cover performance. When I asked what I needed to prepare, I was told there was nothing I needed to prepare in advance. My position is that the scrutiny and escalation around that period were not coincidental.”

### 3. If asked why the 23 January meeting was unfair or significant

“My understanding was that the meeting was to discuss my flexible working arrangements. Instead, it became a formal performance discussion with termination risk. My notes record that there had been no proper formal process or performance framework put in place after September, but on 23 January management relied heavily on a short January period and on informal Jira estimates as if they were authoritative performance benchmarks.”

### 4. If they say it was just ordinary performance management

“I understand that is their position. My response is that I am not saying employers can never manage performance. I am saying that, in my case, the timing, the way earlier complaint discussions were later characterised as performance discussions, the lack of notice for the 23 January escalation, and the later hours restriction all create legal risk beyond ordinary performance management.”

### 5. Forced resignation script, about 45 to 60 seconds

“I accept that I resigned, but my position is that it was not a genuinely voluntary resignation. After I raised protected matters and made a childcare-based flexible work request, I was placed into a termination-risk review process. During that review period I was told my performance would only be assessed on work completed within ordinary business hours. In practice, that removed flexibility I had been relying on to manage care of my child and put me in a worse position than when I made the request. At the same time, the ongoing pressure was having a serious effect on my health. In those circumstances, continuing in the role was not realistically possible.”

### 6. If you want to add the medical point

“By that stage I was experiencing serious work-related anxiety symptoms, including panic attacks, chest pains, and repeated time off. My doctor and psychologist can corroborate that. I rely on that as part of the overall picture showing why continuing in the role was not realistically possible.”

### 7. If they say your contract already required ordinary hours

“The contract does refer to ordinary hours, but it also contemplated variable start and finish times by agreement, and in practice flexibility had already been discussed and used in connection with my family responsibilities. My point is that the review-period restriction removed that flexibility at the worst possible time, after I had made the childcare-based request.”

### 8. Settlement transition

“I understand there are factual disputes and that this forum is for resolution, not final findings. From my perspective, the documents create enough litigation risk on workplace rights, adverse action and dismissal to justify a sensible commercial resolution today.”

### 9. If no agreement is reached

“I understand. In that case I would seek the certificate and consider the next step.”

## Two short fallback lines

If you get flustered:

“I’m not trying to argue every factual dispute today. I’m explaining why the employer faces real risk if this proceeds.”

If they press you on resignation:

“My position is not that I casually chose to leave. My position is that the employer’s course of conduct left me with no real practical choice.”
