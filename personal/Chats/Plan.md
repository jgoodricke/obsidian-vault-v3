Personal Safety, Parenting and Property Plan

Core aim

The goal is not to “win” against my wife. The goal is to protect my son, protect myself, stop coercion, preserve my legal position in the home, and move from threats and pressure into formal, enforceable arrangements.

The best long-term outcome may involve not living together anymore, but that does not automatically mean I should leave the house first or suddenly. Leaving the relationship, leaving the property, parenting arrangements, mortgage responsibility, and property settlement are separate decisions.

Guiding rules

1. I will not sign anything transferring ownership of the house or changing major financial/property rights unless I have independent legal advice.
2. I will not agree to my son travelling overseas unless I have legal advice and a written arrangement that protects his return.
3. I will avoid confrontation, threats, yelling, blocking exits, grabbing phones, following, or any behaviour that could be misrepresented.
4. I will document facts, not insults or character judgments.
5. I will speak to professionals before taking visible action that could escalate the situation.
6. If I or my son are in immediate danger, I will call 000.

Immediate steps: next 24 to 48 hours

1. Speak to a family lawyer or Victoria Legal Aid urgently

I should contact Victoria Legal Aid Legal Help on 1300 792 387, or a private family lawyer/community legal centre, and say:

“My wife has threatened to take our son to Korea without my consent. He already has an Australian and Korean passport. She has also threatened to make false police allegations if I tell anyone about the abuse, and she is pressuring me to sign over the house. I need urgent advice about the Family Law Watchlist, surrender of passports, interim parenting orders, an intervention order, and protecting my interest in the property.”

Victoria Legal Aid’s phone line is listed as 1300 792 387, Monday to Friday, 8 am to 6 pm. Safe Steps provides 24/7 specialist support in Victoria on 1800 015 188.

2. Ask specifically about urgent child travel orders

Because my son already has a Korean passport, a passport alert alone may not be enough. The Australian Passport Office says a child alert will not stop a child travelling if they already have, or are entitled to, an Australian or foreign travel document.

I should ask a lawyer about urgent orders that:

- prevent my son being removed from Australia
- require both his Australian and Korean passports to be surrendered
- stop either parent applying for replacement passports or travel documents
- request that the AFP place him on the Family Law Watchlist
- set temporary parenting arrangements

The Federal Circuit and Family Court says that where there is a threat a child may be removed from Australia, the Court can make orders restraining removal, requiring passports to be delivered to the Court, and requesting placement on the Airport Watch List.

The AFP Family Law Watchlist alerts police when certain children leave Australia, but generally requires court documents that limit or prevent overseas travel and request the AFP to place the child on the Watchlist.

3. Create a secure evidence folder

I should create a secure folder she cannot access. This could be a new cloud account with a password she does not know and two-factor authentication, or a trusted person holding copies.

I should collect:

- screenshots of threats about taking my son to Korea
- screenshots of threats about false police reports
- screenshots or notes about pressure to sign over the house
- mortgage documents, title/property documents, rates notices and bank records
- my son’s birth certificate
- photos or copies of both passports, including passport numbers and expiry dates
- recent photos of my son
- details of any Korean family addresses or likely destinations
- childcare, GP, Medicare, maternal health and local community records showing my son’s life is based in Australia
- a dated timeline of incidents

The tone of the record should be factual. Example:

“9 July 2026, approx. 8:30 pm, kitchen. She said that if I told anyone, she would tell police I abused her. She referred to the incident from two years ago where I raised my voice after being berated for a long period. I did not touch or threaten her. I left the conversation.”

4. Make a safety plan with a support service

I should contact Safe Steps or 1800RESPECT and make a safety plan. 1800RESPECT describes safety planning as thinking about ways to stay as safe as possible while experiencing domestic, family or sexual violence, and says support services can help create a plan.

My safety plan should cover:

- where I can go in an emergency
- who I can call
- what documents I need ready
- how to leave with my son safely if there is immediate danger
- what to do if police attend
- how to keep my phone, passwords and location data safe
- how to avoid escalating conversations

5. Do not confront her with the plan

I should not tell her I am considering legal advice, a Watchlist application, or an intervention order until I have legal advice. If there is a real risk of her leaving with my son, warning her may increase the risk.

Steps for this week

1. Get formal legal advice on four linked issues

The advice should cover:

1. Child travel risk: Watchlist, passport surrender, no-travel orders.
2. Parenting: interim parenting arrangements, handovers, communication, and what to do if she withholds my son.
3. Family violence: whether I should seek a Family Violence Intervention Order, and what conditions would be appropriate.
4. Property: how to protect my interest in the home and avoid signing anything under pressure.

In Victoria, a Family Violence Intervention Order can protect a person, children and property, and conditions can include no contact, not living in the same home through an exclusion order, not damaging property, not approaching certain places, or not using surveillance.

2. Prepare for possible false allegations

I should prepare a short factual statement in case police attend:

“I have not abused my wife. I am afraid because she has threatened that if I tell anyone what is happening, she will tell police I abused her. She has also threatened to take our son to Korea and pressure me over the house. I have been keeping a dated record because I am worried about being misidentified. I want the full history and context considered.”

Victoria Police recognises the issue of identifying the predominant aggressor and says police should consider history, context, harm, fear, self-defence or violent resistance, and who is most in need of protection.

3. Speak to a GP, psychologist or counsellor

I should make a confidential appointment and explain what has been happening. This creates a dated professional record and gives me support. I should be honest and specific, including the one incident where I raised my voice and then collapsed crying.

The goal is not to present myself as perfect. The goal is to be credible, accurate and calm.

4. Contact the mortgage lender’s hardship team or a financial counsellor

I should not assume the only choices are “pay mortgage plus rent” or “stay trapped”. Moneysmart says lenders must consider financial hardship assistance when asked, and options can include payment plans, altered repayments or temporary deferral.

I should also consider calling the National Debt Helpline on 1800 007 007 for free financial counselling. Moneysmart lists the National Debt Helpline as a free service for people overwhelmed by money or personal issues.

5. Identify one trusted person

I should choose one safe person who can know the basics. I can say:

“I’m dealing with serious coercive control and threats involving my son and the house. I’m getting legal advice. I may need you to hold copies of documents or be someone I can call if things escalate.”

Next 2 to 6 weeks

1. Put child protection orders in place if advised

If legal advice supports it, I should seek urgent orders preventing overseas travel, requiring surrender of passports, and placing my son on the Family Law Watchlist.

This is the most urgent legal priority because once a child leaves Australia, recovery may involve international processes. The Hague Convention provides a lawful procedure for seeking the return of abducted children to their home country, but prevention is usually safer and faster than recovery.

2. Formalise parenting arrangements

I should move towards written, formal arrangements for:

- where my son lives
- when each parent spends time with him
- how handovers occur
- whether either parent can travel with him
- how major decisions are made
- how communication happens

This removes some of the power from threats like “I’ll take him away unless you do what I say”.

3. Decide whether staying in the home is safe

There are several possible paths:

- I remain in the home temporarily with legal protections.
- She leaves voluntarily.
- She is excluded from the home by an order, if the court considers that appropriate.
- We separate under one roof for a period.
- I leave later, but only after parenting, property and financial protections are in place.
- The house is sold or refinanced later as part of property settlement.

The key point is that I should not assume I must leave immediately just because I am the person seeking help.

4. Consider Services Australia implications

If we separate but continue living under one roof, Services Australia has a specific “Separated under one roof” process. Services Australia says that if requiring the other person to complete the form would put someone at risk, the other person does not need to complete it.

This may matter for family payments, child support, and financial planning.

Medium-term plan: 2 to 6 months

1. Move from informal conflict to formal structure

The medium-term goal is to replace threats and uncertainty with:

- court orders or written agreements
- safe communication channels
- documented parenting arrangements
- clear property negotiations
- financial hardship or refinancing options
- legal advice before major decisions

2. Begin property settlement planning

I should not treat the house as something my wife can demand. Property should be handled through family law advice and settlement.

Since 10 June 2025, family law property changes expressly recognise economic or financial abuse within the family violence framework and make the effect of family violence relevant in property and financial division after relationship breakdown.

Possible property outcomes include:

- one person buys the other out
- the house is sold and equity divided
- one person stays temporarily while parenting and finances are sorted
- refinancing occurs
- court orders stop improper transfer, sale or disposal of assets

3. Set boundaries around communication

If safe and legally appropriate, communication should move to written channels. This may mean text, email, a parenting app, lawyers, or limited child-focused communication only.

I should avoid long verbal arguments, especially at night or when emotionally exhausted.

Long-term plan: 6 to 18 months

The likely long-term future is not necessarily “I walk away from the house”. A healthier long-term future could look like this:

1. My son remains safely in Australia unless both parents or a court agree otherwise.
2. Parenting arrangements are written down and enforceable.
3. My wife cannot use threats about Korea, police, or the house as leverage.
4. The mortgage and house are dealt with through legal property settlement.
5. Communication is limited, structured and child-focused.
6. I have a stable place to live, whether that is the current home, a refinanced arrangement, a rental, or a new place after sale.
7. My son has a predictable relationship with me.
8. I have emotional and legal support, rather than handling everything alone.

What success looks like

Success does not require destroying my wife or proving everything all at once.

Success looks like:

- I am no longer isolated.
- I have legal advice.
- My son cannot be taken overseas without protection.
- I have a factual evidence record.
- I have not signed the house away under pressure.
- I have a safety plan.
- I know what to do if police attend.
- I have a financial plan for the mortgage.
- Parenting and property issues are handled through proper channels.

What I should avoid

I should avoid:

- signing property documents under pressure
- agreeing to overseas travel without legal advice
- warning her about urgent legal steps if there is a flight risk
- retaliating, threatening, yelling, or physically blocking her
- secretly installing tracking, spyware or surveillance tools
- destroying or hiding passports without legal advice
- relying only on verbal conversations
- assuming police or courts will automatically understand the full context without records

First three calls to make

1. Victoria Legal Aid or a family lawyer: urgent advice about child travel, parenting, property and false allegations.
2. Safe Steps: safety planning around staying in the home and possible escalation.
3. Mortgage hardship team or National Debt Helpline: options if separation affects mortgage payments.

Personal reminder

I do not need to solve the whole future today. The first stage is to move from fear and isolation into advice, records, safety planning and legal protection. The long-term goal is a stable, safe life with my son, not an impulsive exit that leaves me legally or financially exposed.
